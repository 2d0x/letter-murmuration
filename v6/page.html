Twelve sonnets by Shakespeare. Every letter here is a bird with a body and weight. It keeps its distance, flies like its seven nearest neighbours and dodges collisions in advance. A falcon circles above the page, and the pictures are obstacles.

About these sonnets

Shakespeare's sonnets are 154 poems, first published in 1609. Each is written in iambic pentameter and rhymes abab cdcd efef gg: three quatrains and a closing couplet1. Twelve of them are here, among them LXXIII about bare boughs where birds sang not long ago, and XXIX about the lark at break of day.

So long lives this, and this gives life to thee

The last line of Sonnet XVIII: each word is larger than the one before, from 0.2 to 4 times the heading size. Mass grows with a letter's area, so giants take the longest to speed up and push the small ones aside.

Flocking rules — Craig Reynolds, 1987. Seven neighbours — from observations of starlings over Rome (Ballerini et al., 2008). Fear of collision grows as the time to it shrinks (Karamouzas, Skinner, Guy, 2014). Bodies are separated as in position-based dynamics for crowds (Weiss et al., 2017). Banked flight above the roost — the StarDisplay model (Hemelrijk, Hildenbrandt); turning waves — Attanasi et al. (2014); the flock's responses to falcon attacks — Storms et al. (2019). The take-off wave was borrowed from the terminal flock cbirds. Obstacle avoidance by a ray cast ahead — Reynolds (1999). Every zombie has its own reaction time and its own detour side, so the crowd doesn't sway in unison.
